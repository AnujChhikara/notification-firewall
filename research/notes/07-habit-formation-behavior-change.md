# 07 — Habit formation & behavior change

How behavior actually changes — so the product changes it *durably* instead of for one novelty week.

## The habit loop
Habits are **cue → routine → reward** loops that, with repetition, become automatic —
triggered by context, run with little conscious thought (Wood & Neal; popularized by
Duhigg). Phone-checking is a fully-formed habit: cue (boredom, notification, a lull) →
routine (open app, scroll) → reward (variable hit). See note 02.

**To change a habit you don't erase it — you keep the cue, change the routine.** Habit
*substitution* beats suppression: give the cue a new, satisfying routine.

## How long does it take? (66 days, with huge variance)
Lally et al. (2010): 96 people, real-world daily behaviors. Median time to automaticity
**≈ 66 days**, range **18–254 days**. Two design-critical facts:
- The **"21 days" claim is a myth** — real habit formation is slower.
- **Missing a single day did *not* derail** the process. → Streak-shaming ("you broke your
  streak!") is anti-scientific and harmful; forgiveness of lapses is correct design.

Implication: the product must survive well past the **3-week novelty cliff** (note 09) —
the science says the habit isn't even formed yet at 3 weeks.

## Fogg Behavior Model: B = MAP
Behavior happens only when **Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt** converge simultaneously
(BJ Fogg, Stanford; *Tiny Habits* 2019).
- **Central insight: increasing _Ability_ (making it easier) beats increasing _Motivation_.**
  Motivation is unreliable and fluctuates (note 06); *ease* is stable and designable.
- **Prompts** are essential — no behavior without a trigger. (The apps we're fighting are
  masters of prompts; we can use good prompts for good behavior.)
- **Tiny Habits:** shrink the desired behavior until it needs almost no motivation, anchor
  it to an existing routine (an implementation-intention cue).

**Two levers, then:** (a) make the *good* behavior high-Ability (frictionless) and
well-prompted; (b) make the *bad* behavior low-Ability (add friction) at the prompt.

## COM-B / Behaviour Change Wheel (Michie et al., 2011)
The rigorous academic counterpart. Behaviour = **Capability + Opportunity + Motivation**.
- **Capability** (physical/psychological skill) — e.g., knowing an if-then plan.
- **Opportunity** (physical/social environment) — **this is where friction & choice
  architecture live**, and it's the most designable by an app.
- **Motivation** (reflective + automatic) — includes the habit/impulse level.

Useful because it says intervening on **Opportunity** (the environment) is legitimate and
often the highest-leverage point — matching everything else in this KB.

## Choice architecture / nudge & friction (Thaler & Sunstein)
Small changes to **how choices are presented** change behavior without removing options:
- **Add friction** to unwanted behavior (extra taps, delay, re-entry, grayscale) — converts
  autopilot into a deliberate decision.
- **Remove friction** from wanted behavior.
- **Change defaults** — the default is what most people do.
This is the theoretical backbone of the One Sec result (note 09): a **self-nudge**.

## Synthesis: the design recipe the science points to
1. **Target the loop's cue and routine**, not the person's resolve.
2. **Make good = easy (high Ability), bad = slightly hard (friction at the prompt).**
3. **Substitute, don't just suppress** — offer a routine for the cue.
4. **Forgive lapses**; design for a 60–250-day horizon, not a 21-day sprint.
5. **Intervene on Opportunity/environment** — the app's real power.
6. **Preserve autonomy** (note 06) so it's a self-nudge, not a jailer.
