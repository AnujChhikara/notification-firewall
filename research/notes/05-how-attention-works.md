# 05 — How human attention actually works

The mechanistic foundation. If we build "with the grain" of these systems, features
become principled instead of arbitrary.

## Attention is not one thing — it's three networks
Posner & Petersen's model (1990; "20 years after," 2011) — the dominant framework:

1. **Alerting** — achieving and maintaining a state of readiness/vigilance. (Thalamic +
   frontoparietal; noradrenaline.) *This is what degrades with sleep loss and fatigue.*
2. **Orienting** — selecting *which* input to attend to; shifting between **bottom-up
   (exogenous)** capture and **top-down (endogenous)** control. *This is the battlefield:
   feeds hijack bottom-up orienting; focus requires top-down orienting.*
3. **Executive control** — resolving conflict, overriding the prepotent/automatic response
   to stay goal-directed. (Anterior cingulate, prefrontal.) *This is the "effortful focus"
   muscle.*

**Design consequence:** distraction is fundamentally a fight between **exogenous capture**
(a notification, a bright autoplay) and **endogenous control** (your intention). Every
persuasive-design trick attacks the orienting network's bottom-up channel. Our job is to
protect top-down control — ideally by *removing the exogenous triggers* rather than asking
the user to out-muscle them.

## Executive function — the three cores
Diamond (2013, *Annual Review of Psychology*; downloaded) + Miyake: executive function
decomposes into three separable-but-correlated cores:

1. **Inhibitory control** — resisting the prepotent/impulsive response (the "just one more
   scroll" reflex); selective attention.
2. **Working memory** — holding & updating goal-relevant info in mind.
3. **Cognitive flexibility** — shifting perspective/task.

Higher-order abilities (reasoning, planning, self-regulation) are built *on top of* these.
**Inhibitory control is the core the attention economy attacks** — every design choice is
engineered to defeat it.

## Working memory is tiny and easily swamped
Working memory has a hard, small capacity. Rapidly-switching, high-density feeds impose
**cognitive load** that swamps it, which is why deep processing collapses under short-form
scrolling (see note 03). You cannot will a bigger working memory; you can only reduce load.

## The task-switching tax (why "multitasking" is a lie)
- Interruption → **~23 min** to refocus (Mark).
- **Attention residue** (Leroy 2009): the previous task keeps running in the background.
- Switching *feels* productive but fragments both tasks. See note 01.

## The positive target state: FLOW
Csikszentmihalyi's **flow** — complete absorption in a single task, where challenge matches
skill. It's the *opposite* of the fragmented, switch-heavy state feeds induce. Flow needs:
a clear goal, immediate feedback, and — critically — **freedom from interruption.**

**This is the state the product should be protecting/creating**, not just "less screen time."
Framing the win as *"unbroken, absorbed time"* rather than *"fewer minutes"* aligns with how
attention actually produces value and satisfaction.

## Directed attention is depletable — and restorable
Attention Restoration Theory (Kaplan & Kaplan, 1989; note 04): directed (top-down) attention
fatigues with sustained use and is **replenished by low-demand/natural environments** — not
by switching to *another* demanding screen. Restoration is a real, designable lever.

## Product implications
- **Kill exogenous triggers** (protect the orienting network) instead of relying on the
  user's executive control to win an unfair fight.
- **Reduce cognitive load / switching**, don't add more UI to manage.
- **Design for flow**: protect unbroken blocks; measure *depth/continuity*, not minutes.
- **Build in restoration**, not just suppression (a break ≠ another feed).
