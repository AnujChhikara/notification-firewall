# 03 — Short-form video, doomscrolling & the ADHD link

## Short-form video ("TikTok brain")
Heavy short-form-video use (TikTok, Reels, Shorts) is **consistently associated** with
weaker attention. Across the literature gathered:

- A 2025 systematic review (medRxiv) links heavy use to attentional disruption, reduced
  executive function, emotional dysregulation, and compulsive use.
- Users watching **1–2 h/day** report worse study concentration; negative association with
  study habits (ERIC EJ1454296).
- Greater TikTok use → poorer **sustained attention** and adverse academic performance
  (narrative review 2019–2025).
- Reported large-scale meta-analytic finding: heavy users score lower on **attention,
  inhibitory control, and working memory** (~100k people — cited widely; verify primary before use).

**Mechanism ("TikTok brain"):** rapid scrolling from one high-reward clip to the next
**conditions the brain to expect quick, high-reward stimuli**, favoring brief attention
bursts over sustained concentration. It's operant conditioning at industrial scale.

**Caveat:** most studies are **cross-sectional / correlational.** We can say heavy use and
poor attention travel together and there's a plausible mechanism; we can't yet say
short-form video *causes* a given individual's decline in a controlled way.

## Doomscrolling
Distinct, studied behavior: **compulsive consumption of negative content.**
- Scoping review (Sharpe et al., 2026; 17 studies): associated with anxiety, depression,
  stress, diminished resilience.
- Sustained by **infinite scroll + algorithmic delivery** (design) and **rumination +
  intolerance of uncertainty** (psychology) — people scroll to resolve uncertainty, which
  paradoxically feeds anxiety and more scrolling.
- Worse during crises/emotionally-charged periods.

## The ADHD link — real but modest and bidirectional
This matters for framing the rebrand, since the user cites ADHD-style distractibility.

**Ra et al. (2018, JAMA)** — 2,587 teens with *no* baseline ADHD, 24-mo follow-up. Each
additional high-frequency digital activity → **OR 1.10** for later ADHD symptoms.
Clear **dose-response**: 4.6% symptom prevalence at 0 activities → 9.5% at 7.

**Thorell et al. (2022, systematic review)** — 28 longitudinal studies. ~74% found
associations, but **effect sizes are small (r<0.30)**, and the relationship is
**reciprocal**:
- **Media → ADHD** (media effects): 63% of studies.
- **ADHD → media** (selection effects — distractible people gravitate to these apps): 53%.
- Associations are stronger for **problematic use** than raw screen time, and shrink when
  controlling for baseline ADHD.

### What to take from this
1. The relationship is **genuine but not deterministic** — digital media is a contributor
   and an amplifier, not a sole cause of ADHD.
2. It's a **feedback loop**: distractibility pulls you toward the apps, and the apps deepen
   the distractibility. A tool that interrupts this loop is well-targeted.
3. **Framing:** don't require or imply a clinical diagnosis. The same mechanisms hit the
   diagnosed-ADHD user and the "digitally distractible" user. Serve both; medicalize neither.

## Product implication
- Short-form feeds and doomscroll surfaces are the **highest-yield targets** — they combine
  the strongest attention-harm evidence with the strongest compulsion mechanics.
- Because the ADHD link is **bidirectional**, breaking the loop has outsized value for
  exactly the user this rebrand targets.
- Lead with the *behavior* (endless scroll, binge sessions), not a diagnosis label.
